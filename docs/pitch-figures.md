# Pitch figures: verification results

The project brief lists seven background statistics and says: *"Verify each
against its original source before presenting."* This is that verification.

**Every one of the seven needed correction, qualification, or a different
citation.** Two contained outright false attributions to papers that do not
say what was claimed. Do not use any figure from the original brief in the form
it was written there.

Method: one agent per claim fetched the primary source, then an independent
adversarial checker re-fetched and tried to break the verdict. Where they
disagreed, the checker won. Several sources (IOPscience) are behind bot
blocking, so some verification rests on arXiv preprints — flagged where so.

## Do not present

- The $100,000-per-Keck-night figure as a claim sourced to Handley, Petigura & Misic 2024. It is not in the paper in any version — the checker grepped the arXiv e-print tarball (zero hits on 100,000 / dollar / USD / monetary; every '$' is a LaTeX math delimiter) and an independent IOPscience fetch returned an explicit 'No'. The number originates in a UCLA Anderson Review staff press brief (Monika Brown, 3 April 2024), which is not a sourced valuation. Either drop it entirely or re-cite it to that press brief and label it as such. A reviewer who opens the paper will find nothing, which discredits everything around it.

- The composite Keck sentence 'weather cost ~21.7% of observing time over eight years AND losses are strongly all-or-nothing'. This welds two different papers over two different datasets, and the halves cannot both be true on one denominator. Lubin's own operational threshold is 'complete loss if above 5 hours', which is LOOSER than their '>75% of a night' bin (75% of a 10-13 hr Mauna Kea night is 7.5-9.8 hr). The '>75%' population must therefore be a subset of the total-loss population, requiring 38% <= 18.6% — impossible. Do not present the composite. Either half is presentable on its own with its own citation, uncertainty and epoch.

- The bare figure '21.7%' without its uncertainty. The paper writes 21.7 +/- 17.4%; the scatter is 80% of the mean. Presenting a spread-dominated quantity as a precise constant is exactly what a reviewer will flag. Also note the paper does not state whether +/-17.4 is month-to-month or night-to-night, and it is clearly not a standard error on the mean.

- 'Strongly all-or-nothing' as a description of the Keck weather distribution. The measured split is 33% of nights losing <25% and 38% losing >75%, leaving ~29% intermediate. 'Bimodal but not binary' is what the source supports. The related Handley et al. 2024 statement ('individual quarters are usually either lost entirely to weather, or not') is explicitly framed by its authors as a belief used to justify a modelling choice, not a measurement.

- The claim that ZTF's public alert stream is 'roughly half' of 363,000, i.e. ~195,000/night. This is an unpublished inference whose own inputs disagree: at Patterson et al.'s stated ~60 kB per alert, the same 8.6 GB median tarball gives ~143,000, not ~195,000 — 39% rather than 54% of the all-programs total. Do not present a public-stream number at all, or present the 143k-195k range explicitly flagged as own-computation.

- Any attribution of the 363,000 figure to Patterson et al. 2019 (ZADS) or Bellm et al. 2019 (System Overview). Both LaTeX sources were downloaded and grepped: zero occurrences of '363'. Patterson says '600,000 to 1.2 million alerts on a full night of observing'; Bellm says only 'will stream one million time-domain detections nightly', which is forward-looking design language written before operations.

- Any citation to 'Kulkarni 2020, Cosmic Census'. No such paper exists; two independent searches returned nothing. The Kulkarni paper carrying the 10% figure is 'Towards An Integrated Optical Transient Utility' (arXiv:2004.03511), and its arXiv comments field literally reads 'Submitted to PASP' — it is an unrefereed preprint with no published version in Crossref.

- Any attribution of '10 million alerts per night' to the LSST Science Requirements Document. The SRD specifies only transN = 10^4 candidate transients per field of view per visit; all five SRD source files were grepped and contain no per-night alert figure. The per-night number is DMTN-102's arithmetic extension. (Rubin's own DPDD makes this same misattribution, so copying the DPDD's phrasing will propagate the error.)

- The researcher's caveat sub-claims that the adversarial checker could not reproduce: that Ivezic et al. 2019 states no SNe/yr number and that the 10^6 SNe/yr figure traces to the LSST Science Book; the PLAsTiCC specifics (7,846-event spectroscopic training set, 3.5 million detected light curves, 4MOST efficiency curve, WFD prescale fractions 0.003/0.008); and the Rubin TVS Roadmap quote (Malanchev et al. 2022). Per the instruction to downgrade anything the checker could not confirm, these are UNVERIFIABLE and should not be presented.

- The statement that '2 of 10 Gurobi instances were infeasible' for Handley et al.'s N=100 full-night case. Table 1 reads NumFeas = 10, NumOptimal = 8: feasible in all ten runs, optimality proven in eight, and the paper states averages are taken over NumFeas. Where the researcher and the checker disagree here, the checker is correct and the researcher's own quoted table row contradicts the researcher's own caveat.

- The Mroz et al. 18% figure presented as current. No re-measurement of the ZTF twilight streak fraction has been published since September 2021. The 18% corresponds to ~1,600 deployed Starlinks; the constellation is now ~11,100 in orbit (planet4589.org, 2026-09-16), past the paper's own ~10,000 threshold for 'essentially all twilight images affected'. Any present-day figure is extrapolation, not measurement, and the extrapolation is not clean (brightness mitigation cuts one way, other constellations the other). Date-stamp it or drop it.

## Reworded, safe to present

- ZTF alert rate. Instead of 'ZTF produces a median of roughly 363,000 alerts per night, sometimes over 1 million', use: 'Per the ZTF team, the median number of alerts per observing night across all ZTF programs is 363,000, and can exceed 1 million on nights observing the Galactic plane (Wang et al. 2024, ApJ 962, 91, Sec. II.1).' Three things are doing work there: 'all programs' (public + partnership + Caltech, so the public stream alone is materially lower), 'median per observing night' (not a full-night rate — Patterson et al. quote 600,000-1.2M for a full night of observing, and both are true because they answer different questions), and the Galactic-plane condition on the >1M nights. If the figure is being used to size peak throughput or worst-case ingest, 363,000 is the wrong number and 1.2M-2M is the right one. Flag that the verification rests on the arXiv preprint; the published ApJ text could not be loaded.

- Rubin/LSST alert rate. Instead of 'projected at around 10 million alerts per night', use: 'Rubin/LSST is designed to handle up to ~10 million alerts per night (10^4 alerts per visit x ~10^3 visits/night), a figure DMTN-102 notes approximately matches the expected scientific yields; the observatory's current public expectation for steady-state operations is ~7 million per night, and early operations in February 2026 produced ~800,000 on the first night of public alerts.' Cite DMTN-102 Sec. 3.3 (and its line-151 statement, which is the single cleanest citation and was missing from the original write-up) plus LSE-163. Do not call 10 million a 'projection' unqualified: DMTN-102 says explicitly, in the sentence right after the number, that 10,000 alerts/visit is 'a requirement on the DMS and not a scientific estimate of the intrinsic rate of transients and variables in the universe.' Also note the nightly total averages a very non-uniform distribution: ~5,100 alerts/visit extragalactic vs ~32,000 Galactic, with a 40,000/visit ceiling requirement.

- Supernova classification fraction. Instead of 'only about 10% of supernovae currently receive spectroscopic classification', use: 'As of around 2020, only ~10% of the ~10^4 transients discovered each year were spectroscopically classified; the fraction has since drifted down to roughly 8% as discovery outgrew flat spectroscopic throughput (TNS: 7.8% in 2025, 8.2% in 2026 to date). Villar et al. 2020 estimate ~0.1% in the LSST era.' Two substitutions matter: 'transients' not 'supernovae' (the ratio's denominator is reported TNS Astronomical Transient candidates, which include CVs, AGN and variable stars), and dropping the bare word 'currently' (the 10% is a 2020-vintage figure). Note the 0.1% is an uncited one-sentence intro estimate with no derivation, and that Kulkarni's independent 'factor of 20 or more' worsening implies ~0.5% — about 5x higher — so 'or less' overstates a source that gives no lower bound. Cite Villar et al. 2020, ApJ 905, 94, Sec. 1 (verified via the arXiv/ar5iv rendering, not the published IOP text).

- WHT overheads. Instead of 'a WHT breakdown found 22% of time lost to observing overhead, with only 50% spent integrating', use: 'A 1998 ING analysis of 462 WHT nights from 1992-94 (C. R. Benn) found observing overheads consumed 31% of usable clear night, leaving 69% with a detector exposing; expressed as fractions of scheduled time, including that era\'s 24% weather and 4% technical losses, that is 22% overhead and 50% integrating.' The 31/69 pair is the portable one and the one the source itself defends ('Observing overheads ~30% of clear time are typical for large telescopes'); the 22/50 pair is a 1990s-weather artifact — ING's current QC figures (~20% weather, ~2% technical) would rescale the same 31% to ~24%. Add that the sample is 65% ISIS medium-dispersion spectroscopy and that ING files the analysis under 'Use of telescope time (pre-WEAVE)', so it is a visitor-mode spectroscopy number from a pre-queue manually-driven control system, not a statement about telescopes today. Also note 'integrating on targets' is the page's label for 'any detector exposing', which includes arc/comparison-lamp calibration, so true science-target integration is somewhat below 50%.

- Traveling telescope. Instead of 'an optimized order for 100 targets cut slewing from over 3 hours to 0.5 hours', use: 'For 100 simulated targets in a 10-hour night, Handley, Petigura & Misic (2024, AJ 167, 33) reduced slew time to an average of 36.1 minutes against a 200-minute random-ordering benchmark, an 82% reduction, or a factor of 5 as stated in their abstract.' The 3 h -> 0.5 h framing is the paper\'s own rounded introduction and implies a factor of 6.7, inconsistent with both the abstract and the measured result; quoting it propagates the paper\'s most optimistic framing. Add that the 200-minute baseline is constructed by design (100 slews x an assumed 2 min average), not observed Keck performance, and that the targets are simulated California Legacy Survey stars with a 1 deg/s slew model in both axes. If a monetary framing is wanted, cite the valuation separately to a Keck/UCO source with a stated year, not to this paper.

- ZTF Starlink streaks. Instead of 'the share of ZTF twilight images with satellite streaks rose from under 0.5% in late 2019 to 18% by August 2021', use: 'Starlink trails appeared in under 0.5% of ZTF twilight images (solar elevation above -18 deg) in late 2019 and 18% by August 2021, with roughly 1,600 Starlinks then in orbit (Mroz et al. 2022, ApJL 924, L30). The authors projected essentially all twilight images affected once the constellation passes ~10,000 satellites — a threshold it has since crossed — though no updated ZTF measurement has been published.' Two qualifiers are load-bearing: this counts Starlink specifically, not all operators, and 'affected' means at least one trail anywhere in the frame (1.09 streaks per affected image; one trail masks ~0.04% of the detector). The authors state plainly that 'the current science operations of ZTF are not yet strongly affected', projecting only ~0.2% of twilight pixels lost even at a full 42,000-satellite constellation — quoting 18% without that sentence overstates the science damage the authors claim. Note also that the authors\' own Conclusions state the trend more modestly as 'from 6% in late 2020 to 18% in 2021 August'.

## The two worst

**`$100,000 per Keck night`, attributed to Handley, Petigura & Mišić 2024.**
The number is not in that paper in any version. The checker grepped the arXiv
e-print tarball: zero hits on `100,000`, `dollar` or `USD`, and every `$` in the
source is a LaTeX math delimiter. It originates in a UCLA Anderson Review press
piece. Citing a peer-reviewed paper for a number it does not contain is the
single most damaging thing on this list.

**`Kulkarni 2020, "Cosmic Census"`.** No such paper exists. Two independent
searches returned nothing. The 10% figure appears in Kulkarni's *"Towards An
Integrated Optical Transient Utility"* (arXiv:2004.03511), whose own comments
field reads "Submitted to PASP" — an unrefereed preprint with no published
version in Crossref.

## The one that is simply stale

The WHT overhead breakdown is real and correctly quoted, but it analyses **462
nights from 1992–94**, published 1998, and 65% of the sample is ISIS
spectroscopy. Presenting it as a current, instrument-generic telescope overhead
figure would not survive a reviewer who clicked the link.
